1What you will learn

  • Two-stage retrieval: fast, broad recall first, then precise reranking.
  • Cross-encoders vs bi-encoders vs late interaction (ColBERT) vs LLM rerankers.
  • How many candidates to rerank, and what it costs.
  • MMR for diversity, and using reranker scores to decide when to abstain.

2Why it matters

First-stage retrieval is tuned for speed over millions of chunks, so its top 5 are often only "related". A reranker reads each candidate together with the question and reorders them by actual relevance. It is usually the single biggest precision gain you can add. Better top-5 chunks mean better answers and fewer tokens.

Why cross-encoders are better

Every query token can attend to every passage token (lesson 04). The model sees that "carry over" in the question matches "may be transferred to the following year" in the passage, and that the passage is about sick leave, not annual leave. Separate vectors can't make that comparison.

4How it works in practice

  1. Take the fused top 30–50 candidates (lesson 16).
  2. Build (question, chunk text) pairs. Truncate long chunks to the reranker's limit, often 512 tokens.
  3. Score all pairs in one batch on GPU (tens of milliseconds) or CPU (hundreds of milliseconds or more).
  4. Sort by score and keep the top 5–10, optionally applying MMR or parent expansion.
  5. Check the best score against a calibrated threshold. If nothing is relevant enough, abstain (lesson 18).

MMR — maximal marginal relevance

next = argmax over candidates d:  λ · rel(q, d) − (1 − λ) · max over selected s: sim(d, s)

MMR picks relevant chunks that are not near-duplicates of chunks already chosen. Use λ ≈ 0.5–0.8. It helps when the top results repeat the same paragraph from several versions or overlapping chunks.

5In the real world

  • Examples of open rerankers: bge-reranker-v2-m3, the Qwen3-Reranker family and mxbai-rerank. Check languages and license (some reranker families are non-commercial).
  • Candidate count is the main cost lever. Reranking 100 pairs costs about twice as much as 50. Measure the recall gain per extra candidate.
  • The reranker can't recover a chunk that first-stage retrieval missed. Recall first, then precision.
  • Reranker scores are model-specific. Calibrate thresholds on your evaluation set, and re-calibrate when you change models.
  • Set a timeout. If reranking fails, fall back to the fused order rather than failing the request.

6Common mistakes

  • Reranking only the top 5 — there's nothing left to reorder.
  • Cutting chunks in the middle of the key sentence when truncating.
  • Reusing thresholds from a different reranker model.
  • Running the reranker on CPU with 200 candidates and wondering why answers take 8 seconds.

8Check yourself

Why not use a cross-encoder for first-stage retrieval?
It needs one forward pass per (query, chunk) pair at query time. Over millions of chunks that is far too slow, so it is only used on a small candidate set.
What problem does MMR solve?
Redundancy: several near-identical chunks filling the top results. MMR trades a little relevance for diversity.
The correct chunk is rank 140 after fusion and you rerank the top 50. What happens?
It is never reranked and never reaches the prompt. Fix recall first (chunking, query, more candidates) — the reranker can't fix it.
