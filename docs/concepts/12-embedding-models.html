1What is a retrieval embedding model?

Interview answer — say this first
In simple words

An embedding model is the model that turns text into vectors (lesson 03). For search, it is trained on millions of "question → correct passage" pairs, so a question and the passage that answers it end up with very similar vectors.

2Why do we need it?

Semantic search only works as well as the embedding model. A good model puts the right passage near the question; a weak model puts unrelated text there. The choice affects quality, languages, speed and memory — and it is hard to change later, because every chunk must be embedded again.

Remember — why it is fast

Each passage is embedded on its own, without the question. So we embed all passages once, store the vectors, and at question time we only embed the short question. That is what makes search over millions of chunks fast.

Analogy: a translator into a "meaning language"

The model translates every text — questions and documents — into the same coordinate system of meaning. It learned this from millions of pairs: "this question" ↔ "this is its correct answer". Each time, it moved correct pairs closer and wrong pairs further apart.

3How does it work?

1

A transformer encoder reads the text

The model is usually an encoder (like BERT). It produces one vector per token.

2

Pooling makes one vector

The token vectors are combined into a single vector for the whole text, usually by averaging them (mean pooling).

3

Questions and passages are encoded separately

This design is called a bi-encoder. Passages are encoded once at indexing time; questions are encoded at search time.

4

Training: contrastive learning

During training, the model sees a question with its correct passage and many wrong passages. It learns to give the correct pair a high similarity and the wrong pairs a low one. Wrong passages that look relevant are called hard negatives and teach the finest differences.

4Example: step by step

How to choose an embedding model

  1. Shortlist from the MTEB / MMTEB leaderboard, using the retrieval scores for your languages.
  2. Check the license. Some top models are non-commercial (for example NV-Embed-v2 and jina-embeddings-v3 are CC-BY-NC).
  3. Check the fit: maximum input tokens, vector dimension, model size, CPU or GPU speed.
  4. Read the model card for the correct prefix or instruction format.
  5. Test 2–3 candidates on your own evaluation questions (recall@k, MRR) and choose.
  6. Pin the version: name, revision, dimension and prefix. Changing it later means re-embedding everything.
Real example: a bilingual (English + Tamil) HR system

The team shortlists three multilingual models with commercial licenses and writes 80 real questions (40 English, 40 Tamil) with known answer pages. They measure recall@10. Model A wins in English but is weak in Tamil. Model B is close in English and much better in Tamil, so model B is chosen and pinned.

Prefixes matter

Many models expect a marker on queries, and sometimes on passages. E5: "query: how many leave days carry over?" and "passage: Employees may carry over…". Nomic: "search_query: …" / "search_document: …". BGE and Qwen3-Embedding: an instruction before the query. Forgetting them silently lowers quality.

5Important terms

Bi-encoder

Encodes the question and the passage separately. Fast, because passages are pre-computed.

Contrastive learning

Training that pulls matching pairs together and pushes non-matching pairs apart.

Hard negatives

Passages that look relevant but are wrong. They teach fine differences.

MTEB

Massive Text Embedding Benchmark: the public leaderboard for embedding models.

Max sequence length

The longest input in tokens. Longer text is cut off.

Matryoshka embeddings

Vectors you can shorten (e.g. 1,024 → 256 numbers) with only a small loss in quality.

Pooling

Combining token vectors into one vector, usually by averaging.

Query prefix

A marker like "query:" that some models need in front of the question.

Remember

Use the bi-encoder to find candidates fast, and the cross-encoder to sort the best few accurately.

7Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • Very fast search: passages embedded once
  • Finds meaning, synonyms and paraphrases
  • Multilingual models match across languages
  • Small models run well on CPUs

Limitations

  • Less accurate than a cross-encoder
  • Weak on exact codes and rare names
  • Changing the model means re-embedding everything
  • Leaderboard scores may not match your domain

9How to explain it in an interview

Your 1-minute answer

DefinitionA retrieval embedding model is a neural network, usually a transformer encoder, that converts queries and documents into vectors for semantic search.

How it worksIt is a bi-encoder: the query and each passage are encoded separately, so passage vectors are computed once and stored. It is trained with contrastive learning on question–passage pairs, using hard negatives, so matching pairs end up close together.

Choosing oneI shortlist from the MTEB retrieval leaderboard, check the license, languages, token limit and size, then test two or three models on our own evaluation questions and pin the exact version and prefixes.

Key pointVectors from different models are not compatible, so a model change means re-embedding into a new index and validating it before switching.

Don't say"We pick the #1 model on the MTEB leaderboard."
Say instead"MTEB gives a shortlist. We check license, languages and size, then test on our own data."
Don't say"The model can be upgraded in place."
Say instead"A new model or revision needs a full re-embed into a new index, validated before switching."
