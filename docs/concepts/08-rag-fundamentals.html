1What is RAG?

Interview answer — say this first
In simple words

RAG means: first search your documents for the parts that answer the question, then give those parts to the LLM and ask it to answer only from them, with sources. It turns a "closed-book exam" into an "open-book exam".

2Why do we need it?

An LLM on its own (lesson 01) has three big problems for business use. RAG fixes all three by putting the right text into the prompt at the moment the question is asked.

LLM alone

  • Has never seen your private documents
  • Knows nothing after its training date
  • Cannot show where an answer came from
  • Guesses (hallucinates) when it doesn't know

LLM + RAG

  • Answers from your own documents
  • Always uses the latest version
  • Every fact has a citation (document + page)
  • Can say "the documents don't contain this"
Analogy: a librarian and a writer

The retriever is a librarian: you ask a question, and they bring the 5 most relevant pages. The generator (the LLM) is a writer: they read those pages and write a short answer, saying which page each fact came from. If the pages don't answer the question, they say so.

3How does it work?

RAG has two parts. The offline part prepares the documents once. The online part runs for every question.

Part A — Offline: indexing (once per document)

1

Parse

Extract text, headings and tables from PDFs, Word files and scans (lesson 09).

2

Clean and add metadata

Remove noise like page footers and duplicates; add labels like department and date (lesson 10).

3

Chunk

Cut each document into small pieces of about 300–800 tokens (lesson 11).

4

Embed and index

Turn each chunk into a vector and store it in a vector index, plus a keyword index (lessons 12–14).

Part B — Online: answering (every question)

5

Understand the question

Rewrite it into a clear search query if needed (lesson 15).

6

Retrieve

Search both indexes and merge the results (lesson 16).

7

Rerank

Re-order the candidates with a more accurate model and keep the best 3–8 (lesson 17).

8

Generate with citations and check

Put the chunks in the prompt, let the LLM answer with citations, and check the answer before showing it (lesson 18).

4Example: step by step

"How many leave days can I carry over?"
  1. Indexing (done earlier): the 40-page leave policy was parsed, cut into about 120 chunks, embedded, and stored in a vector index and a keyword index.
  2. Embed the question with the same embedding model.
  3. Search: get the top 20 chunks by meaning and the top 20 by keywords, and merge them.
  4. Rerank and keep the best 5. Chunk 47 ("Employees may carry over up to five days…") is #1.
  5. Build the prompt: rules + the 5 chunks with source numbers + the question.
  6. Generate: "You can carry over up to 5 days [1], and they must be used by 31 March [2]."
  7. Check that the citations exist and support the claims, then show the answer with links to the pages.

5Important terms

Corpus

All the documents the system can search.

Chunk

A small piece of a document. It is the unit that is searched and cited.

Index

A data structure that makes search fast (vector index, keyword index).

Retriever

The part that finds the relevant chunks.

Generator

The LLM that writes the answer from the retrieved chunks.

Top-k

How many search results are kept, for example the top 5.

Grounding

Making sure the answer is based only on the retrieved text.

Citation

A link from a sentence in the answer to its source chunk and page.

Abstention

Saying "the documents don't contain this" instead of guessing.

Context window

The space in the prompt for the chunks, the question and the answer.

7Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • Uses private and up-to-date knowledge
  • Answers come with sources
  • No model training needed
  • Access control: users only see documents they are allowed to
  • Fewer hallucinations

Limitations

  • Answer quality depends on retrieval quality
  • Bad parsing or chunking breaks everything after it
  • Weak at "summarize all documents" questions
  • More moving parts to build and monitor
  • Adds search time to every request

Remember — how to debug a wrong answer

Check in this order: 1) Is the answer in the documents? 2) Is the right chunk in the top-k results? 3) Did it reach the prompt? 4) Did the model use it correctly? Fix the first step that fails. Most failures are retrieval failures.

9How to explain it in an interview

Your 1-minute answer

DefinitionRAG, or Retrieval-Augmented Generation, combines information retrieval with a language model to answer questions from a specific set of documents.

How it worksOffline, documents are parsed, cleaned, split into chunks, embedded and indexed. When a question arrives, the system retrieves the most relevant chunks with hybrid search, reranks them, and adds the best ones to the prompt. The model then answers only from those chunks and cites them.

WhyIt gives the model private and current knowledge, provides verifiable citations, supports per-user access control and reduces hallucination, all without retraining.

Key pointMost RAG failures are retrieval failures, so when an answer is wrong I first check whether the right chunk was retrieved, then whether it reached the prompt, and only then the prompt and model.

Don't say"RAG means storing documents in a vector database."
Say instead"Vector search is one part. RAG = retrieve relevant text + generate an answer grounded in it, with citations."
Don't say"If the answer is wrong, we improve the prompt."
Say instead"First check retrieval: is the right chunk in the top-k? Most failures are retrieval failures."
Don't say"Long-context models make RAG unnecessary."
Say instead"Long context is costly and slower, misses details in the middle, and has no per-user access control."
